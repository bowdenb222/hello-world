Plant family — a hard rule

Diseases and nematodes live in the soil and attack whole families, not single crops. Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant and potatoes are all nightshades and all feed the same pathogens. Leave three years before a family returns to the same ground. This is the rule worth actually following.

Shading — physics, not opinion

The sun stays in the southern sky, so every plant throws its shadow north. A row of corn or okra on the south side will shade everything behind it. The app flags a row when something noticeably taller sits south of it, close enough to matter.

Companion planting — treat with suspicion

Most "these two love each other" pairings are folklore repeated for a century without evidence. A few effects are real: French marigold grown as a full cover crop suppresses root-knot nematodes, fennel genuinely stunts its neighbours, and tall crops can provide useful afternoon shade. Companion suggestions here are marked as traditional. The family rule above is the one backed by evidence.

Get a soil test before anything else

UGA Extension runs soil tests through your county office for a small fee. Georgia red clay is usually acidic and low in phosphorus, and guessing at lime and fertiliser wastes more money than the test costs. Blueberries need pH 4.5–5.5, which fights every other crop in the garden — that alone is a reason to test.
